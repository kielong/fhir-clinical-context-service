# Research: Clinical Context Packet Service

This note is for planning, not implementation. It explains the scenario this project simulates, the healthcare workflow it sits in, and the design constraints that follow from that context. Technology details are included only where they change a later decision.

No code. No architecture choice yet. After this, we plan.

---

## 1. What this project is

On paper: a small FHIR + local-LLM service.

In practice: a miniature **customer engagement**. A health plan has a scoped problem (manual chart assembly before prior-authorization review) and wants a working solution deployed inside its own cloud environment. The scope is intentionally incomplete: a real customer agreement would say "assemble clinical context from our FHIR feed and show provenance." It would not specify payload shape, which FHIR resources to include, how to handle missing data, or which model to use. Those are decisions the implementer has to make and defend:

1. Read the customer's pain (manual chart assembly before prior-auth review).
2. Make scoped, defensible technical decisions.
3. Ship something a reviewer can actually use.
4. Be able to explain every line, whether it was written by a person or generated by AI.
5. Say what comes next, and stop.

Azure is treated as in scope, as the customer-cloud deployment target. AI coding assistance is used throughout; every line must be explainable.

**Implication:** Optimize for a small system that can be walked through line by line, not for feature count. The README's "what I would do next" section is part of the product.

## 2. Who Autonomize is, and why this problem exists

Autonomize AI (Austin, founded 2022) sells healthcare-native **agentic workflows** to health plans, providers, and life sciences. Flagship problems: medical and pharmacy prior authorization, appeals and grievances, care management, claims, HEDIS / risk adjustment, and document intake.

Their public language is consistent:

- Turn unstructured records (faxes, PDFs, notes, claims, FHIR) into structured, decision-ready context.
- Ground every output in evidence, not model inference alone.
- Keep a human in the lead. Hard constraints prevent high-risk auto-actions. Complex cases escalate with reasoning intact.
- Full audit trails. Every agent action should be traceable, reviewable, and validatable. Their governance framework is called **ACER** (AI Controls for Ethical and Responsible AI).
- Match the *right model to the right task*. Clinical summarization, policy interpretation, and document extraction are different jobs. They talk about routing across frontier LLMs, small domain models, and private fine-tunes — not one model doing everything.
- Evaluate against healthcare ground truth (clinician-validated PA decisions, nurse-reviewed assessments), not generic LLM benchmarks. Four evaluation dimensions they publish: decision accuracy, reasoning quality, operational performance, safety/policy compliance.

Claimed production numbers (useful as *their* framing, not as claims we should copy into the service): ~55% reduction in PA review time, ~76% auto-intake approval rate, ~18 minutes saved per case, 98% accuracy in clinical data abstraction, 95% in clinical contextualization. Prior auth that used to take 20–30 minutes shrinking to seconds is a talking point they use.

The platform pieces they sell around this:

| Piece | What it is, in plain language |
| --- | --- |
| Knowledge Center | A living graph of clinical, regulatory, and coverage concepts |
| AI Marketplace | Prebuilt agents and workflow templates |
| AI Studio | Visual builder for agentic workflows, connectors, evals, human checkpoints |
| Command Center | Ops view: traces, handoffs, exception rates, FinOps |

**Implication for us:** This project is *not* Autonomize's platform. It is a slice of one workflow those agents perform: **assemble the patient's clinical context and cite the record**. If our service starts approving or denying authorizations, we have misunderstood the product.

## 3. The real workflow: prior authorization

### What prior auth is

A **prior authorization (PA)** is a payer (health plan) requiring approval *before* a service, procedure, device, or medication is covered. The provider submits clinical justification. A utilization-management (UM) nurse or physician reviewer checks the chart against medical policy / clinical guidelines and returns approve, deny (with reason), or "need more information."

Today a large share of that intake is still messy: faxes, PDFs, portal dumps, EHR screenshots. Someone has to reconstruct "who is this patient, what do they have, what are they on, what is missing" before the actual policy decision can start. That reconstruction is the slow, manual work this service is meant to reduce.

Autonomize's PA workflow, as they describe it, is roughly:

1. **Ingest** — faxes, medical records, claims, FHIR, X12 278, unstructured docs.
2. **Extract / abstract** — pull clinical facts (conditions, meds, labs, procedures).
3. **Contextualize** — assemble a case a reviewer can scan.
4. **Evaluate against policy** — compare facts to guidelines. *Out of scope for us.*
5. **Recommend with explanation** — still not a final determination for high-risk cases.
6. **Human oversight** — licensed clinicians review denials; audit-ready package.

Our service is steps 2–3, plus a two-sentence scan summary. The requirement is explicit: **the service never issues a final authorization.**

### Why "source" is the one field they refuse to drop

A UM reviewer (and later an auditor, an appeal, or CMS) must be able to click from any fact back to the record. Autonomize's public PA brief says every decision should include guideline citations, evidence context, and plain-language rationale, packaged for audit and appeals. AHIP's 2025 reform commitments, which Autonomize maps their product to, include transparency of determinations and physician oversight of medical-necessity denials.

If a summary says "diabetes" and the source `Condition/1a2b` is actually a resolved ankle sprain, the product is unsafe even if the prose sounds clinical.

**Implication:** Facts and sources should be assembled in code from FHIR. The model should write prose *from those facts*. If the model is allowed to invent conditions, medications, or sources, we are doing the opposite of what Autonomize sells.

## 4. Regulatory backdrop (enough to talk about, not enough to implement)

### CMS-0057-F

The CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule (CMS-0057-F) is the policy wind Autonomize is selling into.

Operational pieces (decision timelines, denial reasons) generally started **January 1, 2026**. FHIR Prior Authorization API requirements are generally **January 1, 2027**. Impacted payers (Medicare Advantage, Medicaid/CHIP, Marketplace QHPs) must:

- Expose a Prior Authorization API that says whether PA is required, what documentation is needed, and accept/respond to electronic requests.
- Return approve (with end date/circumstance), deny (with a *specific* reason), or request more information.
- Meet turnaround: **72 hours urgent / 7 days standard**.
- Make PA status/decisions available through the Patient Access API.

CMS *encourages* (does not strictly mandate a single IG) the Da Vinci Burden Reduction stack:

| Guide | Job |
| --- | --- |
| **CRD** (Coverage Requirements Discovery) | At order time, is PA required for this patient + service? |
| **DTR** (Documentation Templates and Rules) | What clinical evidence does the payer need? Prefill from the EHR. |
| **PAS** (Prior Authorization Support) | Submit the FHIR request bundle, including supporting clinical resources. |

PAS bundles wrap a Claim plus supporting info (`Condition`, `MedicationRequest`, `Observation`, `Procedure`, `ServiceRequest`, `QuestionnaireResponse`, `DocumentReference`, etc.), each pointed to from `Claim.supportingInfo`. That is the industry's formal version of "a packet with sources."

Drugs are largely **out of CMS-0057-F's PA API**. Pharmacy PA is a separate (and Autonomize-sold) workflow. The example packet mixes diabetes + metformin, which looks more like **medical PA clinical context** (or a generic chart packet) than a pharmacy ePA transaction.

### What this does *not* mean for v1

We should not implement CRD/DTR/PAS, X12 278, SMART auth, or a Claim resource. That is out of scope for v1. Knowing the names matters because:

- It explains why FHIR + source citations are the core of the project.
- It gives us language for "what I would do next": scope the packet to a requested service, attach supportingInfo, keep a human checkpoint before any determination.

HIPAA applies to real PHI. Synthea data is synthetic (not de-identified real data), so this laptop project is not a HIPAA system. In a customer VPC it would be. Worth mentioning in "what I'd do next" (auth, audit logs, no PHI in prompts/logs, private model hosting) rather than building now.

## 5. Domain objects we will actually touch

### FHIR, in one paragraph

**FHIR** (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) is the current HTTP/JSON way health systems exchange data. A **resource** is one record: `Patient`, `Condition`, `MedicationRequest`, `Observation`, and so on. Each has an id. After it lives on a server, the canonical pointer is `ResourceType/id` — exactly the `source` field in the example payload.

A **Bundle** is a wrapper of resources. Synthea exports one **transaction** Bundle per patient: POST the whole file to the FHIR base URL and the server creates every contained resource atomically (all or nothing).

### HAPI FHIR

**HAPI** is the open-source Java FHIR server almost everyone uses for local and many production FHIR stores. Official image: `hapiproject/hapi`. Default FHIR base: `http://localhost:8080/fhir`. There is a tester UI at `http://localhost:8080/`.

Default HAPI uses an in-memory or local file DB depending on config; **data dies when the container dies** unless we attach Postgres (common compose pattern). For a demo, persistence is a quality-of-life choice: loading ~1,180 bundles is slow enough that you do not want to redo it every reboot.

HAPI can rewrite resource ids on ingest. Synthea bundles use `urn:uuid:...` internal references. After upload, the ids a reviewer can fetch are **HAPI's ids**, not necessarily the UUIDs in the original JSON files. The example `patient_id` looks like a Synthea UUID (`2fa15bc7-8866-461a-9000-f739e425860a`). In the public sample that UUID is the Patient's Synthea identifier (`Patient.identifier`, system `https://github.com/synthetichealth/synthea`), not the `Patient.id` written inside the file, and HAPI assigns yet another id when the bundle is posted. We will need a clear rule: accept FHIR `Patient.id`, Synthea identifier, or both, and always return sources that resolve on *this* server.

Useful query shapes later (not implementing now):

- `GET /Patient/{id}/$everything` — whole chart. Can be huge.
- `GET /Condition?patient={id}`
- `GET /MedicationRequest?patient={id}`
- Search params such as `clinical-status=active` (conditions) and `status=active` (meds).

`$everything` is convenient and dangerous. Synthea patients often carry **hundreds** of Observations (vitals, labs) across a lifetime. Dumping that into a 3–4B model context window will either truncate, hallucinate, or time out. That is a planning issue, not a "use $everything because it exists" issue.

### Synthea and the "1,180 patient bundles"

**Synthea** (MITRE) simulates entire synthetic lives from census demographics and disease modules. Output is realistic-looking, not real. No PHI, no IRB. Default export: FHIR R4, one transaction Bundle per patient. Newer Synthea releases also write separate `hospitalInformation*.json` and `practitionerInformation*.json` files (organizations, locations, practitioners shared across patients); the public 2019 sample below does **not** include them.

Official sample downloads: [synthea.mitre.org/downloads](https://synthea.mitre.org/downloads). The dataset called "the sample set" is the **"1K Sample Synthetic Patient Records, FHIR R4"** (`synthea_sample_data_fhir_r4_sep2019.zip`, ~81 MB zipped, ~1.3 GB extracted).

Why 1,180 if it is labeled 1K? Synthea's `-p 1000` targets **1,000 living patients**. People who die during the simulation are also exported. The sample contains exactly **1,180 patient bundles**: 1,000 living patients plus 180 who are deceased (they carry a `deceasedDateTime`). The zip holds nothing else, so the file count and the patient count are both 1,180. That is the 1,180 patient bundles this project loads.

**No load order is needed for this sample.** Newer Synthea releases write separate hospital and practitioner files that must be loaded first, but this sample has none: every bundle is a self-contained transaction that includes the Organization, Practitioner, and Encounter resources it references (addressed by `urn:uuid:` ids that the server rewrites on ingest). Two consequences: the same Organization appears in many bundles, so the server ends up holding duplicate Organization and Practitioner resources (expected and harmless here), and every entry is a plain `POST`, so loading a bundle twice duplicates it.

Each patient file typically includes some mix of: Patient, Encounter, Condition, Observation, MedicationRequest, Procedure, Immunization, AllergyIntolerance, CarePlan, Claim, DiagnosticReport, and others. Not every patient has every type. Young healthy patients can have almost no meds or problem list. Elderly patients can have a noisy lifetime of resolved conditions and completed prescriptions.

**Implication:** "Missing" is a first-class output because Synthea (and real EHRs) are uneven. A child with no medications is not a system failure; it is a true gap relative to what a reviewer might expect.

### What the example facts actually are

| Packet field | FHIR resource | What a reviewer thinks it means | Gotchas in Synthea |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| conditions | `Condition` | Diagnoses / problem list | `clinicalStatus` can be active, recurrence, relapse, inactive, remission, resolved. Lifetime "history of X" will drown a summary if we include everything. `verificationStatus` (confirmed vs entered-in-error) also exists. |
| medications | `MedicationRequest` | Orders / prescriptions | Status: active, completed, stopped, cancelled, draft, on-hold. Completed old courses look like current therapy if unfiltered. Display may be coded (RxNorm) plus text. There may be `Medication` resources too; Synthea often inlines the drug on the request. |
| summary | *generated* | Two sentences a reviewer can scan | Must not introduce facts not in the packet. |
| missing | *computed* | What a reviewer would still have to go hunt for | Should be deterministic: empty lists, no labs, no allergies, deceased, etc. Not the model guessing. |
| source | `ResourceType/id` | Fetch this on the FHIR server and confirm the line | Must resolve after HAPI ingest. |

A reviewer assembling a PA chart would often also want, if time allowed: allergies, recent encounters, key labs (e.g. A1c for diabetes), procedures, and the requested service. The example payload only shows conditions + medications. Extra sections are worth adding only if they stay sourced and small.

### FHIR Provenance vs `source`

FHIR has a formal `Provenance` resource (who/what/when produced a record). US Core expects `_revinclude=Provenance:target` on many searches. Synthea can emit Provenance (the 2019 sample contains none), but it is metadata about *how the synthetic record was generated*, not "this condition came from this chart line."

The packet's `source: "Condition/1a2b"` is **a pointer to the clinical resource itself**, which is what a reviewer needs. Building a full Provenance graph would be extra and easy to get wrong. Mention it as a next step for customer EHR data lineage, not as v1.

## 6. Where the LLM comes in

The model is **not** the clinical brain of this service. It is a writer at the end of a deterministic pipeline.

### The path of one request

```
Reviewer (or demo client)
    → GET packet for patient_id
    → Our service queries HAPI for Patient, Condition, MedicationRequest, …
    → Code turns resources into {display, source} lists and computes `missing`
    → Code sends those compact facts to Ollama (local 3–4B model)
    → Model returns two sentences of prose (as JSON, via structured outputs)
    → Service stitches facts + missing + summary into the response
```

The reviewer still sees conditions and medications that came from FHIR, each with a `source` they can open on HAPI. The only field the model is allowed to create is **`summary`**: two sentences a UM reviewer can scan instead of reading raw FHIR JSON.

The four building blocks are HAPI, the Synthea data, the packet endpoint, and the local model summary. The first three are infrastructure and data. The LLM does not load Synthea, does not run HAPI, and does not decide which facts exist.

### Why a model at all

A packet of coded conditions is evidence. A reviewer still has to *read*. Two grounded sentences ("Adult with active type 2 diabetes on metformin; no other active medications on file") save the first 20 seconds of chart-staring. Autonomize's product language calls this clinical contextualization / summarization — a different job from extraction and a different job from policy decision.

Local Ollama (no GPU, no paid API) is also the customer-deployment story: in a payer VPC, chart data often cannot leave the boundary. Hosting a small model next to HAPI is a toy version of "the model runs where the customer's data lives." Structured outputs (`format` + a Pydantic schema) are how we keep that prose in valid JSON instead of markdown wrapped in apologies.

### What the model must not do

| Done in code (system of record) | Done by the model (language only) |
| --- | --- |
| Fetch patient, conditions, meds (and any other included types) | Write a 2-sentence reviewer summary *from the lists we pass in* |
| Attach `source` (`Condition/abc`, `MedicationRequest/xyz`) | Stay inside those facts — no new diagnoses, meds, or ids |
| Compute `missing` from empty/absent resource sets | Return JSON, typically `{ "summary": "..." }` |
| Filter (active vs historical, recency, size cap) so the prompt fits | — |

If we hand the model raw FHIR and ask it to "extract the packet," a 3–4B laptop model will invent conditions, drop sources, and fail the 10-patient check. Autonomize's own writing says agents should retrieve real information and generate outputs grounded in evidence rather than relying solely on model inference. The design keeps that split explicit.

Do not ask the model to re-emit conditions, medications, or sources. The packet is already structured. Structured outputs wrap **the summary**, not the whole payload. If Ollama is down or returns invalid JSON, the service should still return the sourced facts and `missing`, and say the summary could not be generated — fail closed on prose, not on evidence.

### Prompt shape (for planning)

Pass compact lines, not resource JSON, for example:

- Conditions: `Diabetes mellitus type 2 (active) — Condition/123`
- Medications: `Metformin 500 mg (active) — MedicationRequest/456`
- Missing: `No AllergyIntolerance resources on file`
- Instruction: two sentences, no facts that are not in the lists, no approval/denial language

Context window is the hidden constraint. A naive `Patient/{id}/$everything` dump will not fit. Filtering and short display strings are how a laptop model stays honest.

### Model choice (decide during build, justify in README)

Candidates: `llama3.2:3b`, `gemma3:4b`, `phi4-mini:3.8b`, `qwen3:4b`. Early public comparisons of this size class (not gospel): Gemma 3 4B and Phi-class models tend to follow JSON schemas more reliably; Qwen3 4B can be verbose and blow a token budget before finishing JSON; Llama 3.2 3B is lighter and faster. Temperature 0. Bake off on a few patients for JSON validity, latency, and whether the summary stays fair. Justify the pick with measurements in the README.

## 7. What "look at ten patients" is really testing

This is not a unit test. It is the same habit Autonomize describes as evaluation against ground truth.

For each of 10 patients, a human (you) should:

1. Call the endpoint.
2. Open each `source` on HAPI (`GET /fhir/Condition/{id}`, etc.) and confirm the display is a fair reading of that resource.
3. Read the summary against the listed facts (and a glance at the raw chart).
4. Note misses: omitted important active problems, invented problems, stale meds treated as current, "no medications" when meds exist, sources that 404, summaries that over-claim ("well controlled diabetes" when there is no A1c in the packet).

README should have counts, e.g. "10/10 sources resolved, 8/10 summaries fair, 2 overstated control / missed resolved-vs-active." Observations beat a perfect-looking table.

Pick a **diverse ten**, not the first ten files: young vs old, sparse vs busy charts, deceased vs living, chronic disease vs almost empty, polypharmacy. Otherwise the QA does not teach you anything.

"Fair" does not mean "clinically complete." It means: a UM reviewer would not be misled.

## 8. Azure and Docker

A compose file and `pyproject.toml` are enough to run the stack locally. **Azure is in scope**, alongside local Compose, because it is the "customer's cloud" story. That is a deploy target, not extra product surface (no PAS engine, no UI platform).

Loading 1,180 bundles into HAPI is the longest mechanical step. The sample is about 527,000 resources (mostly Observations, Claims, Encounters, and ExplanationOfBenefits) and the largest single bundle is ~42 MB, so expect it to be slow; measure it rather than guess. Persistence (Postgres volume) and a seed script that is **idempotent or skippable** matter more than a Kubernetes chart. Plan for the same seed path locally and in Azure.

Ollama on a GPU-less Azure VM will be slow; Azure OpenAI would fight "no paid API" and the local-hosting story. Prefer running the same small model in the environment (container or VM) unless we later document a hard constraint.

In-scope quality bar (these are core, not extras):

- Data-flow diagram in the README.
- Deterministic `missing` + sourced facts; model only summarizes.
- Ten-patient eval table in the README.
- Model justification (short bake-off: latency, JSON validity, summary fairness).
- Code provenance notes (human vs AI vs OSS).

Do not ship:

- Auto-approve / deny.
- Multi-agent framework for one endpoint.
- Full PAS/CRD/DTR implementation (that is a next-step story).
- A heavy UI you cannot defend.
- Sending chart data to a hosted paid API.

## 9. Questions the design must be able to answer

A reviewer, customer engineer, or auditor will reasonably ask:

- Walk me from HTTP request to FHIR calls to prompt to response.
- Why is `source` attached in code rather than by the model?
- What happens if HAPI is down / patient missing / model returns invalid JSON?
- Why this model? What did you measure?
- Active vs historical conditions — what did you choose and why?
- Would this still work if the customer had Epic/Cerner instead of HAPI? (Answer: same FHIR queries, different auth, data quality, and id schemes.)
- How would you deploy this in a payer VPC? PHI in logs/prompts? Model inside the boundary?
- Show me a patient where the summary was *not* fair. What would you change?
- Where did each function come from: written by a person, generated by AI, or adapted from open source?

If those cannot be answered from the code that ships, the system is overbuilt or under-understood.

## 10. Decisions

The scope already answers the mechanical questions.

**Settled**

- **Synthea set:** public 1K FHIR R4 sample (1,180 patient bundles: 1,000 living patients plus 180 deceased).
- **Deploy:** Azure is in scope, in addition to local Docker Compose.

**Packet scope (settled for v1)**

**v1 is a patient packet (A).** The endpoint takes `patient_id` only. Output is this person's chart excerpt: conditions, medications, a two-sentence sketch of *the patient*, and gaps in the chart as a whole ("No medications on file"). Same packet whether anyone later requests an MRI, a wheelchair, or nothing. That is what the example JSON specifies.

**Request-scoped packets (B) are explicitly out of v1.** They are the first item in §11 (what I would do next). Do not add an optional `service` query in v1. A UM nurse reviews a *request*, not a person, but doing B without medical policy is theater and blows the scope. The README should explain B and why it is the natural sequel.

### Decide and document

| Topic | Why it is ours to choose | What "good" looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patient identifier | FHIR `id` vs Synthea UUID vs MRN | Accept the id the demo can copy-paste; document how it maps; sources must 404-check on HAPI |
| Active vs historical | Unfiltered Synthea problem lists are noisy | A stated rule (e.g. active conditions + active/on-hold meds, plus a count of excluded historical items) |
| Extra resource types | Labs, allergies, procedures help PA but expand scope | Include only if sourced and if the summary still fits two sentences |
| `missing` definition | Not specified | Deterministic gaps vs the packet we claim to assemble (no meds, no conditions, no allergies if we include them) |
| Model | A justification, not a favorite | Short bake-off; structured outputs; laptop-feasible |
| How many patients to load | The full sample is 1,180 bundles; a demo may work on a subset while the rest loads | Prefer full load if time; if not, load a representative subset, make the rest a documented follow-up, and be honest |
| Auth / PHI controls | Real customer need; not in the four pieces | §11 next-step, not v1 |
| UI | The core requirement is one endpoint | API + README/demo script is enough; a thin HTML page is optional sugar |
| Retry / fallback if JSON invalid | Small models still fail | Validate with Pydantic; one retry; fail closed with facts still returned and summary error noted |
| Code citation | Required | README section: OSS (HAPI, Synthea, FastAPI, Ollama), AI-assisted files, human-written files |

## 11. What I would do next (README fodder)

Each item is something a real engagement would do after a thin go-live, with a *why*. None of this is v1 scope.

### Product / workflow (closer to Autonomize PA)

1. **Request-scoped packets (B) — first sequel.**  
   Accept patient + requested service (CPT, Rx, or free text). Rank or filter facts that bear on that request. Redefine `missing` as documentation still needed for *this* PA (e.g. no recent lumbar MRI, no documented PT trial), not "chart has no meds." Rewrite the summary as "why this person is here for this service."  
   **Why:** A UM nurse is never asked to summarize a member in the abstract. Same patient, two PAs, two packets. This is the shortest path from this v1 to the production workflow. Needs a service identifier and relevance rules; Synthea has no PA request objects, so we would invent a small request model or take a query parameter and keep determinations out.

2. **Criteria checklist, still no determination.**  
   Given a service, list the medical-policy bullets a reviewer usually checks (conservative therapy, imaging recency, comorbidity), mark each as present/absent with sources.  
   **Why:** Autonomize's next agent step after contextualization is policy evaluation. Shipping a checklist plus human checkpoint shows we understand the line: assemble evidence, do not authorize.

3. **Richer sourced sections.**  
   Add allergies, recent encounters, procedures, and a few high-yield labs (A1c, eGFR) — each still `{display, source}`.  
   **Why:** Conditions + meds are the minimum. Reviewers also look at allergies (safety), last visit, and labs that speak to control. Keep lists short or the scan packet dies.

4. **Unstructured intake alongside FHIR.**  
   Accept a fax/PDF/clinical note, extract candidate facts, and require each to land on a FHIR resource or be labeled `unverified`.  
   **Why:** That is Autonomize's real differentiator. This project starts from a clean FHIR feed; customers do not.

5. **Da Vinci CRD / DTR / PAS shape.**  
   Optionally wrap the packet as PAS `supportingInfo` on a Claim-like bundle; use DTR-style questionnaires for `missing`.  
   **Why:** CMS-0057-F is the sales wind. We would speak the industry's packet format without pretending we implemented a payer API.

6. **Human review UI.**  
   Thin page: packet, clickable sources (proxy to HAPI), summary, missing, "needs more info" vs "ready for clinician." No approve/deny button.  
   **Why:** v1 is one endpoint; a review surface is the natural demo after the API.

### Trust, eval, model

7. **Groundedness check on the summary.**  
   Parse claims in the two sentences and require each to map to a sourced fact; otherwise regenerate or strip.  
   **Why:** The 10-patient table is manual. This is how you keep summaries fair over time.

8. **Larger eval set and regression.**  
   Beyond ten hand reviews: gold packets, JSON-schema fail rate, latency p95, "summary invented a fact" rate, source 404 rate.  
   **Why:** Autonomize evaluates agents on decision accuracy, reasoning quality, ops, and safety — not a single happy-path demo.

9. **Model routing.**  
   Keep a small local model for summarization; do not use it for extraction (code) or for policy (separate, later). Re-bake-off when hardware or customer residency rules change.  
   **Why:** Their platform talk is "right intelligence for the right task." We already split extraction vs prose in v1; routing makes that explicit.

### Customer-cloud hardening

10. **AuthN/Z, audit logs, PHI-safe prompts.**  
    SMART-on-FHIR or customer SSO; never log full charts; redact prompts; retain who requested which patient.  
    **Why:** Synthea is not PHI. A payer VPC is. This is table stakes the day after the prototype.

11. **Point at a real EHR FHIR API.**  
    Same queries against Epic/Cerner/US Core, plus their auth, id schemes, and messy `clinicalStatus`.  
    **Why:** HAPI + Synthea is the lab. The job is customer connectors.

12. **Bulk load / FHIR Bulk Data / $import.**  
    Replace the 1,180 sequential POSTs with a documented bulk path and resume-on-failure.  
    **Why:** Seed time is the worst part of local demo; customers have millions of resources.

13. **Observability.**  
    Trace: FHIR fetch time, prompt tokens, model latency, schema retries, empty-chart rate.  
    **Why:** Analog of their Command Center; you cannot operate an agent you cannot see.

14. **Packet cache and async summary.**  
    Return facts immediately; attach summary when the model finishes (or job id).  
    **Why:** 3–4B on CPU in Azure will be the slowest hop. Reviewers can read the list while prose generates.

15. **FHIR Provenance / cross-payer lineage.**  
    When facts arrive from another payer or a document, keep `Provenance` as well as `ResourceType/id`.  
    **Why:** CMS payer-to-payer APIs care about data origin. Our v1 `source` is "which resource on this server," which is enough for Synthea and not enough for continuity-of-care.

### Explicitly not next (wrong line)

- Auto-approve or auto-deny.
- A general-purpose multi-agent graph with no new user job.
- Training or fine-tuning a model on Synthea (synthetic, wrong distribution, wrong eval).

When writing the README section, pick a short ordered list (B first, then checklist, then EHR/auth) rather than dumping all fifteen. The value is in the sequence, not a backlog dump.

## 12. Design implications to carry into planning

These are constraints, not a proposed architecture.

1. **Evidence path is deterministic; language path is generative.** If a fact has a `source`, a GET to HAPI should prove it. The model never becomes the system of record.

2. **The product user is a UM reviewer, not a clinician at the bedside and not an autonomous agent.** Two-sentence scan, missing-data list, clickable sources. No determination, no confidence theater that implies a decision.

3. **Filter before summarize.** Lifetime Synthea charts will not fit a 3–4B window and will not be scannable. Recency and clinical-status rules are product decisions.

4. **HAPI ids after ingest are the sources that matter.** Plan for identifier mapping (the Synthea identifier is not the HAPI id). Each bundle is self-contained, so no separate hospital/practitioner load order is needed.

5. **Loading 1,180 bundles is an engineering problem, not a footnote.** Persistence, a seed job, and a way to verify `Patient?_summary=count` belong in the plan.

6. **Evaluation is part of the deliverable.** Ten hand-reviewed patients with counts in the README are required.

7. **Small is a feature.** Prefer a boring compose + FastAPI + Pydantic + Ollama path. Azure is in scope as a deploy target, not as extra product surface. Request-scoped packets (B), PAS, and auth live in §11.

8. **Cite code origins as we write**, so the README is not a scramble at the end.

9. **LLM is summarization only.** Plan the endpoint so facts and `missing` exist even if Ollama is unavailable.

## 14. After the build: what a self-review measured and changed

The build was finished and reviewed once as a whole, against the assignment, before anything was
submitted. What was measured, what was decided, and what was left alone on purpose.

### Measured first

- **Coverage** was 99%; the two uncovered spots were the branch that rejects a birth date that
  looks complete but is not a real day (`2020-13-45`), and the cached settings loader. Both now have
  tests, and each test was shown to fail when the line it guards is broken. Coverage of `src/` is 100%.
- **Dead code:** a search for unused code found none. Every setting is read somewhere and is
  documented in `.env.example`.
- **History:** no secrets, `.env` files or data in any commit.
- **Types:** with the Pydantic plugin fully on, mypy found 19 real errors, mostly untyped helpers and
  fields that were `str` where the model wants a `Literal`. None was a runtime bug; all are fixed and
  mypy now runs in `make lint` and in CI.
- **Wider lint rules** (naming, simplification, comprehensions, performance) found 11 things: five
  exception classes not ending in `Error` and six loops or `try/pass` blocks. Fixed, and the rules stay on.

### Other ways to use structured outputs

The summary already has to be exactly `{"summary": "..."}`. Three further shapes were tried or weighed:

- **A schema that forces a citation per sentence** (each sentence carries the source ids it rests on,
  limited by an `enum` of the ids in the prompt). Ollama enforces the shape, including `enum`,
  `minItems`/`maxItems` and `maxLength`. But at 4B the model **cites everything it was shown** rather
  than what a sentence uses, so the citations prove nothing, and the output was about twice as many
  tokens. Not adopted: a citation the model cannot get right is a false assurance, which is worse than none.
  The check that would work (every claim traces to a listed fact) is on the "next" list, and it belongs in code.
- **A two-sentence array or a length limit in the schema.** Rejected: the checks already enforce
  two sentences, and a schema that cuts a sentence at N characters produces broken prose.
- **LangChain or LangGraph around the model call.** Rejected: a dry-run install adds 24 packages;
  it does not make a temperature-0, fixed-seed model any more deterministic (the wording differed
  with Ollama's prompt-cache state, and only remembering the finished summary fixed that); and it
  would put a framework between the code and the one pinned request that decides the output.

### Changed

- **Package layout.** `fhir/` (talks to HAPI), `packet/` (models and assembly, pure), `llm/` (the only
  model use), `routers/`; the tests mirror it. The dependency direction is one way:
  routers, then packet and llm; `fhir` stands alone.
- **Type checking** in the lint gate and CI.
- **The error contract is documented**, not only implemented: 404, 409, 422, 502 and 500 are declared
  in the OpenAPI with one shared body model, FastAPI's default 422 body (which echoes the rejected
  input) is no longer advertised, and a test checks that each documented example is the body actually sent.
  Packets are sent `Cache-Control: no-store`.
- **A lockfile** for the runtime dependencies. The container installs from it; a test checks that it
  pins every dependency to a version `pyproject.toml` allows, and that the Dockerfile installs the
  lock before it copies the code, so the dependency layer is rebuilt only when the lock changes.
- **Exception names** end in `Error`.

### Left alone on purpose

- An app factory instead of a module-level `app`: it would not change behaviour, and the tests
  already isolate the app through dependency overrides.
- Regrouping `scripts/`, splitting `llm/checks.py`, renaming `eval_ten.py`: each is one file or one
  command, and moving them would only make the history harder to follow.
